Section 1 · Before cracks

A hole concentrates stress by a factor Kt

Start with a regular problem: a wide plate in tension with an elliptical hole, semi-axis a across the load and b along it. The radius at the end of the hole is ρ = b²/a. Inglis (1913) [1] gave the exact peak stress:

(1)Kt = σmax / σ∞ = 1 + 2a/b = 1 + 2√(a/ρ) A circular hole (b = a) gives the familiar Kt = 3. The field in the figure is the exact Kolosov–Muskhelishvili solution [2]. Source: Inglis [1]; full field Muskhelishvili [2] · Notes §2.1–2.2

Kt depends only on the shape. Scale the hole up or down and σmax does not change. Now let the hole flatten into a crack: as b → 0, ρ → 0 and Kt → ∞, so the stress concentration factor stops being useful. One product stays finite, though:

(2)KI = limρ→0 (√π / 2) σmax √ρ = σ∞ √(πa) Substituting Inglis: (√π/2)σmax√ρ = σ∞[√(πa) + √(πρ)/2]. The second term vanishes for a sharp crack. Source: Irwin [5] · Notes §2.3

The stress intensity factor is the limiting case of the stress concentration problem. It keeps the information Kt loses, and it brings in a size effect through √a: a longer crack is more dangerous even with the same shape.

Section 2 · Why we need K

A sharp crack makes the elastic stress infinite

A plate with a through crack of length 2a is pulled with a uniform remote stress σ∞. The colour is the opening stress σyy. Far from the crack it equals σ∞; at the tips it concentrates without limit.

(3)σyy(x, 0) = σ∞ x / √(x² − a²) Westergaard solution [3] on the crack line. x is the distance along the crack line measured from the crack centre, so the crack occupies −a ≤ x ≤ a and the tips are at x = ±a. Valid for |x| > a; it tends to infinity as x → a. Source: Westergaard [3] · Notes §3.1

Watch the probe walk towards the tip. Halving the distance does not double the stress, it raises it by about √2. A failure criterion based on "the stress at the tip" is therefore meaningless for a sharp crack. What stays finite is the strength of the singularity, and that is the stress intensity factor K.

Section 3 · The shape of the singularity

Every mode I crack tip has the same field; only K changes

Close to the tip, in polar coordinates (r, θ) centred on it, the leading term of the Williams expansion [4] is the same for every cracked body under mode I loading:

(4)σij = KI / √(2πr) · fij(θ) fyy(θ) = cos(θ/2)[1 + sin(θ/2) sin(3θ/2)], fxx(θ) = cos(θ/2)[1 − sin(θ/2) sin(3θ/2)], fxy(θ) = cos(θ/2) sin(θ/2) cos(3θ/2) Source: Williams [4]; Irwin [5] · Notes §4

The geometry, the crack length and the load all enter through one scalar, KI, in MPa√m. The probe circle shrinks towards the tip: the stress climbs as 1/√r (slope −½ on the log plot), yet σ·√(2πr) ÷ f(θ) stays fixed at the applied K.

Move your pointer or finger over the figure to place the probe anywhere. Try θ = 0 for σyy: there fyy = 1, so K = σyy√(2πr) directly. τxy vanishes on the crack line, so it cannot give K there.

Section 4 · Calculating K by hand

K = Y σ √(πa), with Y from the geometry

For standard geometries the full elastic problem has already been solved, and the answer is tabulated as a dimensionless geometry factor Y [7] (also written F or β).

  1. Identify the geometry and loading, and measure a the way the handbook defines it (half-length for a centre crack, full depth for an edge crack).
  2. Read Y at the current a/W. Check that you are inside its range of validity.
  3. Convert a to metres, so that K comes out in MPa√m.
  4. Multiply out K = Y σ √(πa).

Section 5 · Calculating K from a numerical model

Extrapolate the apparent K back to the tip

When no handbook solution exists you model the part, often with finite elements, and read the near-tip results. Each node gives an apparent K by inverting the singular term at its own distance r:

Higher-order terms of the Williams series [4] make the apparent K drift linearly with r, so you fit a straight line and take its intercept at r = 0. The nodes nearest the tip are unreliable because ordinary elements cannot reproduce 1/√r. Slide the fit limit to 0 and watch the intercept go wrong.

The data here are synthetic: the exact Westergaard field for a 2a = 40 mm centre crack at σ = 120 MPa, with a mesh error added near the tip. In practice the J-integral [13] (K = √(J E′)) is the more robust route, because it is evaluated away from the tip. See the FE software page for quarter-point elements and a live spider-web mesh.

Section 6 · Using K

Fast fracture when K reaches KIC

Because K fully describes the near-tip field, two cracks with the same K are in the same state, whatever the component. That is why a single material property, the plane-strain fracture toughness KIC, can predict failure:

(6)KI = Y σ √(πa) ≥ KIC  ⇒  ac = (1/π)(KIC / Yσ)² Geometry, stress and the current crack length come from section 4. When Y depends on a/W, solve for ac numerically. Source: Irwin [5]; Anderson [9] · Notes §7.1

The check is only valid while the plastic zone is small compared with the crack and the part. Irwin's estimate [5] is rp = (1/π)(K/σys)² in plane stress, about a third of that in plane strain.

Toughness and yield values are typical handbook figures for teaching. Use certified data for design.

The small-scale yielding principle

A real metal cannot carry infinite stress, so it yields in a small zone at the tip. LEFM still works because of small-scale yielding: if that plastic zone is small compared with the crack length, the ligament and the thickness, it sits inside a ring where the elastic K field is still accurate. That ring sets the boundary conditions on the plastic zone, so what happens inside it, and whether the crack grows, is controlled by K alone. Two cracks with the same K have the same plastic zone and fail at the same K, which is why a KIC measured on a small specimen applies to a large structure.

(7)ry = (1/2π)(K/σys)²  →  rp = 2ry = (1/π)(K/σys)² First estimate from σyy = σys at r = ry, then Irwin's force balance doubles it (plane stress). Plane strain: about (1/3π)(K/σys)². Dugdale strip yield [8]: (π/8)(K/σys)². An effective crack length a + ry can be used to correct K. Source: Irwin [5]; Dugdale [8]; Anderson [9] · Notes §7.3

The rules of thumb are rp ≲ a/50 for an accurate K, and σ ≲ 0.8σys on the net section. A valid plane-strain KIC (ASTM E399 [10]) needs a, B and W − a all ≥ 2.5(KIC/σys)², about 25 plastic zones. When these fail, the regime is large-scale yielding and the J-integral or CTOD replaces K (see the J-integral page). In the small-scale yielding limit, J = K²/E′. Section 7 · Through the thickness

K and the plastic zone change from the surface to the mid-plane

A 2D analysis gives one K for the crack. In a plate of thickness B the crack tip is a line, the crack front, and the state changes along it. In the interior the surrounding elastic material stops the tip region from contracting in z, so σzz ≈ ν(σxx + σyy): plane strain. On the free surfaces σzz = 0: plane stress. The constraint ratio Tz = σzz / ν(σxx + σyy) goes from 1 to 0 across a surface layer whose depth grows with the distance r from the front [15].

(8)Tz(r, z) ≈ 1 − cosh(z/r) / cosh(B/2r) A simple boundary-layer form used for the figure: plane strain where r ≪ B, plane stress where r ≳ B, and always plane stress on the surfaces. The banded map above shows it in the crack plane, with contour lines every 0.2; the hatched orange area is the plastic zone. Source: trend from 3D FE, Nakamura & Parks [15]; plate theory, Kotousov & Wang [17]

Local K. 3D analyses find K nearly uniform in the interior, a few percent above the 2D value, and falling in a thin layer next to each surface. On the surface itself the singularity is no longer 1/√r but r−0.45 for ν = 0.3, the corner singularity of Bažant and Estenssoro [16], so K as defined in eq. (1) formally vanishes there. The left graph uses an illustrative profile K(z) ∝ 1 − (ν/2) e−s/0.06B, with s the distance to the nearest surface, scaled so its average is the 2D K. At ν = 0 the 3D effect disappears.

Plastic zone. Under triaxial constraint a higher opening stress is needed to yield. Interpolating Irwin's constraint factor m between 1 (plane stress) and √3 (plane strain) [5] gives, at each z:

(9)rp(z) = (1/π) [K(z) / m σys]²,   m = 1 + (√3 − 1) Tz(rp, z) Solved numerically at each z because Tz depends on rp. m = 1 recovers eq. (7) in plane stress; m = √3 gives the plane-strain value (1/3π)(K/σys)². Source: Irwin [5]; Anderson [9] · Notes §7.3

The result is the "dog-bone" zone of the textbooks [9]: small in the interior, two to three times larger near the surfaces. Press play: as K rises the surface lobes grow, and once rp becomes comparable with B, plane stress spreads through the whole thickness. Thin sheets therefore show a higher apparent toughness Kc than KIC and slanted shear lips on the fracture surface. ASTM E399 asks for B ≥ 2.5(KIC/σys)² [10] so that the plane-stress surface layers are a small part of the front. The higher K at mid-thickness is also why fatigue precracks tunnel, with a front curved forward in the middle.

In a CT specimen. The 3D view shows the lower half of a standard compact tension specimen (ASTM E399 [10], W = 2B, a/W = 0.5) with the upper half removed, so you look at the crack plane. The orange lobes around the front are the plastic zones of the K field, one slice every few z, drawn in the space where the upper half was (they are symmetric about the crack plane) and magnified by the factor shown. "Show K ribbon" adds the local K(z) from the left graph as a ribbon standing on the crack front, coloured from low (blue) to high (red). The load P that gives this thickness-average K comes from the E399 calibration:

(10)K = P / (B√W) · f(α),   f(α) = (2 + α)(0.886 + 4.64α − 13.32α² + 14.72α³ − 5.6α⁴) / (1 − α)3/2 α = a/W; f(0.5) ≈ 9.66. This is the 2D (thickness-average) K; the ribbon spreads it along the front with the profile of the left graph. Source: ASTM E399 [10]; Tada, Paris & Irwin [7]

Each lobe is the von Mises yield boundary of the K field, σe = σys, with the local K(z) and σzz = Tz ν(σxx + σyy) from eq. (8):

(11)r(θ) = (K² / 4π σys²) [1 + cos θ + (3/2) sin²θ]   (plane stress)
r(θ) = (K² / 4π σys²) [(1 − 2ν)²(1 + cos θ) + (3/2) sin²θ]   (plane strain) Between the surfaces and the mid-plane the figure uses the general Tz. In plane strain the lobes lean back and nearly vanish ahead of the tip (θ = 0); at the surfaces they are large and round. These first-order lobes ignore load redistribution, so along θ = 0 they are smaller than eq. (9). Source: Anderson [9] · Notes §7.3

Cuts through the plastic zone. The last figure cuts the zone in the x–y plane at the position chosen with the slider (purple slice in the 3D view, orange curve here) and compares it with the two limits: plane stress at the surfaces (solid) and plane strain at the mid-plane (dashed), normalised by (1/π)(K/σys)². The same von Mises construction works for the other modes with their near-tip fields (see the angular functions page). In mode II the zone is larger, with lobes ahead of and behind the tip, and it shrinks less in plane strain. Mode III is antiplane shear: σzz = 0, so the zone is the same circle in both states, r = (3/2π)(KIII/σys)², centred on the tip.

Because K is highest at mid-thickness, a fatigue precrack in a CT specimen grows fastest there and its front bows forward, which is why E399 measures the crack length at several points through the thickness and limits the curvature of the front.

This is a teaching model: eq. (8) and the K(z) profile are simple fits to the trends of 3D elastic FE, and eq. (9) is Irwin's first-order estimate at each z without 3D redistribution. For numbers, run a 3D elastic–plastic FE model (see the FE software page).
