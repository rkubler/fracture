K and J in finite element software

How commercial FE codes model a crack, why the mesh at the tip needs special treatment, and how K and J are extracted from the results. The central figure is a real finite element solution, computed in your browser, on a spider-web mesh you can change.

Section 1 · The workflow

What an FE code needs to know about a crack

A crack is a cut in the mesh with two traction-free faces that meet at a tip (2D) or a front (3D). The faces share coordinates but not nodes, so they can open. All mainstream codes (Abaqus [17], Ansys [18] and others) follow the same steps.

  1. Define the crack. Mark the crack faces as a seam (duplicate nodes along the faces) or build the cut into the geometry. Define the tip or front and the direction of virtual crack extension, the vector q (along x in the notation of the other pages).
  2. Mesh the tip region with a focused "spider-web" mesh. Concentric rings of elements around the tip, with the innermost ring made of collapsed quadratic elements whose mid-side nodes are moved to the quarter points (Section 2). Rings make it easy to define integration domains.
  3. Solve. A linear elastic analysis for K, or an elastic–plastic one (small or finite strain) for J.
  4. Extract fracture parameters. Request J, KI, KII, KIII and the T-stress over several contours (rings). Codes use the domain integral for J and the interaction integral for K and T (Section 4); displacement extrapolation and the virtual crack closure technique are alternatives.
  5. Check. Values should be the same on all contours except the first one or two. If they drift, the mesh or the model is wrong.

Section 2 · The singularity problem

Polynomial elements cannot follow 1/√r, unless the mapping is distorted

Near the tip the displacements vary as √r and the strains and stresses as 1/√r (equations of Williams [12] and Irwin [13]). Ordinary elements interpolate displacements with polynomials, so their strains stay finite at the tip. Refining the mesh helps only slowly, and the nodes closest to the tip are always the least accurate.

Henshell and Shaw [1] and Barsoum [2] found a remarkably simple fix. Take an ordinary 8-node quadratic element and move the mid-side nodes on the sides that touch the tip from the middle to the quarter point. Along such a side, of length L, the isoparametric mapping becomes

(1)x = L(1 + ξ)²/4  ⇒  ξ = 2√(x/L) − 1 so the quadratic interpolation u = a0 + a1ξ + a2ξ² becomes u = b0 + b1√x + b2x, and ε = du/dx contains b1/(2√x). Source: Henshell & Shaw [1]; Barsoum [2]

The figure shows one element side with nodes at 0, mL and L, carrying the exact √x displacement at its nodes. With m = ½ the strain is finite at the tip. With m = ¼ the Jacobian dx/dξ vanishes at the tip and the element reproduces the 1/√x singularity exactly. Below ¼ the Jacobian changes sign inside the element, which is not allowed.

No new element type or special code is needed: the singularity comes from where you put the nodes. This is why every major FE code uses quarter-point elements for LEFM.

Section 3 · A spider-web mesh, solved live

Change the mesh and watch K and J respond

This is a genuine finite element analysis, run in your browser each time you change a setting. It uses the standard boundary-layer model: a disc of radius R = 10 mm around the tip, with the exact mode I displacements for KI = 50 MPa√m imposed on its edge, plane strain, E = 200 GPa, ν = 0.3. Only the upper half is meshed, with symmetry on the ligament (uy = 0), and the crack face is free. The exact answer everywhere inside is the Williams–Irwin field, so every error you see is a mesh error.

  1. Elements. 8-node quadratic quadrilaterals (Q8), integrated with 3×3 Gauss points. The innermost ring is collapsed: the three nodes on the side facing the tip are merged into one tip node, which turns each quadrilateral into a triangle.
  2. Quarter points. The orange nodes are the radial mid-side nodes of the tip ring. Switch between "Standard Q8" (mid-side at L/2) and "Quarter-point Q8" (at L/4) and compare the error map near the tip.
  3. Displacement extrapolation. The lower chart plots the apparent Kapp(r) from the crack-face opening, equation (2), and extrapolates it to r = 0 from the nodes of the first element.
  4. Domain J. Each bar is J from one ring of elements, equation (5). The highlighted ring on the mesh is the domain of the selected contour.

Things to try. With standard elements the extrapolated K is about 9–10% low and refining the mesh barely helps, while J from the outer contours is within about 0.5%. With quarter-point elements the extrapolated K is within about 1% even on a coarse mesh. Contour 1 is always the least accurate J, because it contains the tip elements. Switch to curved sides: the quarter-point element loses most of its advantage (K about 10% low again), because the singular mapping of equation (1) needs straight sides from the tip. Reduce the sectors to 2 and every result degrades, because the angular variation of the field is no longer resolved.

J is accurate even with poor tip elements because the domain integral weights the solution away from the tip, where the mesh is good. This is the practical reason codes report J (and K via the interaction integral) rather than nodal stresses or displacements.

Section 4 · Extraction methods

Four ways to get K or J from an FE solution

Displacement extrapolation

Use the opening of the crack faces, v(r) for one face, and the near-tip solution [15]:

(2)Kapp(r) = (2μ/(κ + 1)) √(2π/r) v(r) κ = 3 − 4ν in plane strain, (3 − ν)/(1 + ν) in plane stress. Plot Kapp against r and extrapolate to r = 0, ignoring the nodes nearest the tip. Source: Chan, Tuba & Wilson [15]; Anderson [11] §12.1

With quarter-point elements, the two face nodes of the tip element (b at L/4, c at L) give K in closed form [4]:

(3)KI = (2μ/(κ + 1)) √(2π/L) (4vb − vc) vb, vc are the opening displacements of one face. With both faces modelled, replace 4vb − vc by [4(vb − vd) − (vc − ve)]/2. Source: Shih, de Lorenzi & German [4]

Virtual crack closure (VCCT)

Irwin's crack closure argument: the energy released by extending the crack by Δa equals the work needed to close it again. With nodal forces at the tip and the opening one element behind it [5]:

(4)GI = Fy Δv / (2 B Δa),   KI = √(GI E′) For 4-node elements of equal size Δa at the tip. Quadratic and quarter-point elements need weighted combinations of corner and mid-side forces. GII, GIII follow from the other force components. Source: Rybicki & Kanninen [5]; Krueger [14]

VCCT needs no singular elements and is popular for delamination in composites.

Domain integral for J

The contour integral of Rice [10] is converted into an area (2D) or volume (3D) integral with a smooth weight q that is 1 at the tip and 0 on the outer edge of the domain [6][7]:

(5)J = ∫A ( σij ∂ui/∂x1 − W δ1j ) ∂q/∂xj dA Evaluated at the Gauss points, where stresses are most accurate. Each ring of elements gives one "contour". In 3D q is a virtual extension of the crack front, and J is obtained point by point along the front. Source: Li, Shih & Needleman [6]; Shih, Moran & Nakamura [7]

Interaction integral for K and T

J gives only the total energy, (KI² + KII²)/E′ + KIII²/2μ. To separate the modes, superpose a known auxiliary field (for example a pure mode I field with K = 1) on the FE field; the cross term of J is [8]

(6)I = ∫A ( σij ∂uiaux/∂x1 + σijaux ∂ui/∂x1 − σik εikaux δ1j ) ∂q/∂xj dA = 2 KI KIaux/E′ Repeat with a mode II auxiliary field for KII. With the auxiliary field of a point force on the crack line, the same integral gives the T-stress. Source: Yau, Wang & Corten [8]

Section 5 · Collapsed-element variants

Where the nodes go decides the singularity

Barsoum [3] showed that the same collapsed 8-node element can represent different singularities, depending on whether the three nodes at the tip are tied together and where the mid-side nodes sit. FE codes expose exactly these two switches.

Tip nodesMid-side nodesStrain singularityUse
Tied¼ point1/√rLinear elastic (LEFM): K, J, T
Free½ point1/rPerfectly plastic material, small strain
Free¼ point1/√r and 1/rElastic–plastic with hardening (the HRR exponent n/(n+1) lies between ½ and 1)
Tied½ pointnoneStandard element, slow convergence

For elastic–plastic J analyses with large deformation, a sharp tip is replaced by a small blunt notch (a "keyhole" with a root radius well below the expected crack-tip opening) and ordinary, well-shaped elements, because the tip blunts and the singular elements would distort badly [16]. J from the outer contours is insensitive to these details.

Section 6 · Practice

Mesh guidelines, 3D crack fronts and XFEM

Checklist for a 2D spider-web mesh

  1. Quadratic elements everywhere near the tip; collapsed quarter-point elements in the first ring.
  2. Straight sides on the tip elements, with the quarter-point nodes exactly on the rays from the tip.
  3. Sector angles of about 10° to 22.5° (8 to 16 sectors over 180°, 16 to 32 around a full tip).
  4. Tip element size L small compared with the crack length, typically a/20 or less, and the K-field zone covered by several rings.
  5. Several rings (4 to 10) of gradually growing size, so that J can be checked on several contours.
  6. Report the converged contour values, not contour 1, and confirm that the spread between contours is small (often < 1–2%).
  7. For plasticity, small-strain: free tip nodes; finite strain: a blunt notch with fine regular elements.

3D crack fronts

The 2D spider web is swept along the crack front: collapsed 20-node bricks become wedges with quarter-point nodes, and each slice of elements along the front is a "tube". J, K and T are evaluated at every node along the front with a 3D domain integral, so the result varies along the front, for example from plane strain in the middle of a thick plate to a lower constraint at the free surfaces [7].

XFEM

The extended finite element method [9] avoids meshing the crack at all. Elements cut by the crack get a discontinuous (Heaviside) enrichment, and elements around the tip get the √r angular functions of the Williams field. The crack can then run through a fixed mesh, which is convenient for crack growth. K is still extracted with the interaction integral; accuracy depends on the enrichment radius and on the quadrature in cut elements.

Linking back

Every FE result here is checked the same way as on the other pages: K against handbook solutions where they exist, and J against K²/E′ for elastic models. See the stress intensity page and the J-integral page.

Section 7 · Doing it in Abaqus

A 2D crack in Abaqus/CAE, step by step

The live model in Section 3 is exactly what Abaqus builds when you follow the steps below: a half model with a symmetry plane, a partitioned spider web, collapsed quarter-point CPE8 elements at the tip, and one J value per ring of elements. Menu names are those of Abaqus/CAE 2017 and later [19][20][21]; recent releases keep the same workflow.

Abaqus/CAE workflow

  1. Part, partitions. With Partition Face: Sketch, draw a small circle centred on the tip (it holds the collapsed ring), a larger concentric circle (the spider web) and radial lines. The partitions make the rings of elements, and each ring becomes one contour.
  2. Crack faces. If the crack lies on a symmetry plane, model half the body and skip the seam (step 4 sets the symmetry). Otherwise use Interaction module → Special → Crack → Assign Seam on the crack line, so that the mesh gets duplicate nodes that can separate [22].
  3. Crack definition. Special → Crack → Create, type Contour integral. Pick the crack front (for a sharp 2D crack, the tip vertex or the small circular region around it), then the crack tip, then the extension direction: q vectors (two points along the crack, pointing into the uncracked ligament) or Normal to crack plane.
  4. Edit Crack, General tab. Tick On symmetry plane for a half model; Abaqus then doubles J (the SYMM parameter). Check that the blue q arrow points ahead of the crack.
  5. Edit Crack, Singularity tab. Midside node parameter = 0.25 and Collapsed element side, single node for LEFM. For plasticity see the tips below.
  6. Mesh. In the circular partition: Mesh Controls → Quad-dominated, Sweep; Abaqus degenerates the first ring into collapsed quadrilaterals. Elsewhere: Structured or Free quads. Element Type: Standard, Quadratic, Plane strain (CPE8 or CPE8R). Seed the circle edges with 8 to 16 elements over 180° and bias the radial edges towards the tip.
  7. Step, history output. Create History Output → Domain: Crack, Number of contours 5 to 10, Type J-integral. Create separate requests for Stress intensity factors (choose the crack initiation criterion: maximum tangential stress, maximum energy release rate or KII = 0) and for T-stress.
  8. Results. Values are written per contour to the .dat file and, with history output, to the .odb (Visualization → Result → History Output, or Report → XY).

The same requests in the input file

For the half model of Section 3, with a node set TIPFRONT containing the tip node TIPNODE and q along x:

*STEP
*STATIC
...
*CONTOUR INTEGRAL, CONTOURS=8, CRACK TIP NODES, SYMM, TYPE=J, OUTPUT=BOTH
TIPFRONT, TIPNODE, 1.0, 0.0
*CONTOUR INTEGRAL, CONTOURS=8, CRACK TIP NODES, SYMM, TYPE=K FACTORS, DIRECTION=MTS
TIPFRONT, TIPNODE, 1.0, 0.0
*CONTOUR INTEGRAL, CONTOURS=8, CRACK TIP NODES, SYMM, TYPE=T-STRESS
TIPFRONT, TIPNODE, 1.0, 0.0
*END STEP

Data line: crack-front node set, crack-tip node (or set), qx, qy (and qz in 3D). TYPE = J, C, K FACTORS or T-STRESS; DIRECTION = MTS, MERR or KII0 applies to K FACTORS only. Without OUTPUT, results go to the .dat file only [23].

XFEM alternative

Special → Crack → Create → XFEM (keyword *ENRICHMENT, TYPE = STATIONARY CRACK) inserts the crack in an existing mesh with no partitions, seam or singular elements [24]. Contour integrals are then requested with CRACK NAME = the enriched feature. Use a locally refined mesh around the tip; the first contours are again the least accurate.

Tips and common pitfalls

TopicAdvice
Singularity settingsLEFM: midside 0.25, single node (1/√r). Perfect plasticity: midside 0.5, duplicate nodes (1/r). Hardening plasticity: midside 0.25, duplicate nodes (1/√r and 1/r). With first-order elements only the duplicate-node option applies [20].
Straight sidesKeep the tip-ring elements straight-sided (Section 3 shows why). Do not let the mesher curve them to follow a partition circle of large radius; a small first circle helps.
Which contours to reportIgnore contours 1 and 2, which contain the tip elements. Report the mean of the converged contours and their spread; a spread above 1–2% means the mesh needs work [25].
q directionA q vector pointing back into the crack gives J < 0. In 3D, check the arrows at every front node, especially on curved fronts.
SymmetryOn a symmetry plane, use the symmetry option instead of a seam. Forgetting it halves J and divides K by √2.
What is available for which materialStress intensity factors and T-stress need a linear elastic (homogeneous or bimaterial interface) model. J is available for elastic–plastic materials and is path independent for monotonic, proportional loading (deformation-plasticity assumption).
UnitsAbaqus has no units. With N and mm, J comes out in N/mm = kJ/m² and K in MPa√mm; divide K by √1000 ≈ 31.6 to get MPa√m.
Large deformationWith NLGEOM and plasticity, replace the sharp tip by a small blunt notch meshed with regular quadratic elements, and use the outer contours.
3D cracksSweep the spider web along the front with C3D20R; the collapsed elements become wedges. J, K and T are given per front node. Reduced integration avoids oscillation of the first contour along the front; values at free-surface nodes are less reliable [25][26].
Step typeContour integrals cannot be requested in a linear perturbation step; use a general *STATIC step.
Check the meshColour the mesh by element type to see the collapsed ring, and query the crack-face nodes to make sure the two faces do not share nodes.
