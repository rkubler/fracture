Measuring KIC in the laboratory

How the plane-strain fracture toughness of a metal is measured on a standard specimen, following ASTM E399: choosing and sizing the specimen, growing a sharp fatigue pre-crack from a machined notch, recording the load–displacement curve, calculating the provisional value KQ, and checking whether it qualifies as a valid KIC. The theory behind each step is on the other pages and is linked where it is used.

Section 1 · The procedure

From a block of material to a valid KIC

KIC is the critical value of the mode I stress intensity factor at which a crack starts to grow under plane strain and small-scale yielding (section 6 of the stress intensity page). Measuring it means loading a cracked specimen whose K is known exactly as a function of the load, and detecting the load at which the crack starts to extend. The test method ASTM E399 [1] fixes every step so that results from different laboratories can be compared. A result is first called KQ, a provisional value, and becomes KIC only if all the validity checks pass.

  1. Choose the specimen and orientation. Compact C(T) or single-edge bend SE(B), with the crack plane identified by a code such as L-T (section 2).
  2. Size it. Estimate KIC, then make B and a larger than 2.5 (KIC/σYS)² (section 2).
  3. Machine the notch. A narrow slot ending in a V or a chevron, with a root radius of 0.08 mm or less (section 3).
  4. Fatigue pre-crack. Grow a sharp crack from the notch at a low, controlled Kmax until a/W ≈ 0.5 (section 3).
  5. Test. Load at a controlled rate while recording load P against crack-mouth opening v with a clip gauge (section 4).
  6. Analyse the record. Draw the 5% secant line and find PQ; check Pmax/PQ ≤ 1.10 (section 4).
  7. Measure the crack. On the broken halves, average the crack length at three points of the front and check its shape (section 5).
  8. Calculate KQ and check validity. KQ from the specimen formula, then size and pre-cracking checks. If they all pass, KQ = KIC (sections 5 and 6).

This page follows the 1990 edition of E399 (reapproved 1997) [1], which is the document used in the course. Later editions keep the same procedure but revise some details, for example the size requirement now also applies to the ligament W − a. Section 2 · Specimens and size

Five standard shapes, one size rule

E399 allows five specimen types. The two used in nearly every laboratory are the compact specimen C(T), a notched plate pulled through pins in two holes, and the single-edge notched bend specimen SE(B), a beam in three-point bending with a span S = 4W. The other three are cut from round products. The disk-shaped compact specimen DC(T) is a C(T) machined from a bar or a forged disk (diameter D = 1.35W). The arc-shaped tension specimen A(T) is a notched ring segment from a tube or a hollow forging, pulled through two holes, with the crack growing radially from the outer surface (C-R orientation). The arc-shaped bend specimen A(B) is a ring segment in three-point bending (Annex A9). In the standard proportions the thickness is B = W/2 and the total crack length (notch plus fatigue crack) is a ≈ B, with 0.45 ≤ a/W ≤ 0.55. For each type, K is known from a calibration that combines the load with a geometry function of α = a/W, exactly the Y factors of section 4 of the stress intensity page:

(1)SE(B): K = P S / (B W3/2) · f(α),   f(α) = 3√α [1.99 − α(1 − α)(2.15 − 3.93α + 2.7α²)] / [2(1 + 2α)(1 − α)3/2] Accurate within 0.5% for any a/W when S/W = 4. f(0.50) = 2.66. Source: ASTM E399 Annex A3 [1]; Srawley [7]
(2)C(T): K = P / (B W1/2) · f(α),   f(α) = (2 + α)(0.886 + 4.64α − 13.32α² + 14.72α³ − 5.6α⁴) / (1 − α)3/2 Accurate within 0.5% for 0.2 ≤ a/W ≤ 1. f(0.50) = 9.66. In SI units, P in kN and B, W in cm give K in MPa√m. W is measured from the load line (the hole centres), not from the front edge. The same formula drives the 3D view in section 7 of the stress intensity page. Source: ASTM E399 Annex A4 [1]; Srawley [7]
(2b)DC(T): K = P / (B W1/2) · f(α),   f(α) = (2 + α)(0.76 + 4.8α − 11.58α² + 11.43α³ − 4.08α⁴) / (1 − α)3/2 Accurate within 0.3% for 0.2 ≤ a/W ≤ 1. f(0.50) = 10.17. As for C(T), W and a are measured from the load line. Source: ASTM E399 Annex A6 [1]
(2c)A(T): K = P / (B W1/2) · [3X/W + 1.9 + 1.1α] · [1 + 0.25(1 − α)²(1 − r1/r2)] · f(α),   f(α) = √α (3.74 − 6.30α + 6.32α² − 2.43α³) / (1 − α)3/2 X is the offset of the load line from the inner surface (X/W = 0.5 for a half ring, 0 for the smallest segment), r1 and r2 are the inner and outer radii, and W = r2 − r1. Here a and W are measured from the outer surface. f(0.50) = 3.73. Accurate within 1% for 0.45 ≤ a/W ≤ 0.55 with X/W = 0 or 0.5. Source: ASTM E399 Annex A5 [1]

The size rule makes sure that the plastic zone is small compared with the specimen, so that the tip is in plane strain and K controls it (small-scale yielding):

(3)B, a ≥ 2.5 (KIC / σYS)² σYS is the 0.2% offset yield strength (ASTM E8 [4]) at the test temperature and rate. 2.5(K/σYS)² is about 50 times the plane-strain plastic zone (1/3π)(K/σYS)². Since KIC is unknown before the test, use a conservative (high) estimate, or the table of minimum sizes by σYS/E given in the standard: from 75 mm for σYS/E ≈ 0.005 down to 6.5 mm for σYS/E ≥ 0.010. Source: ASTM E399 §7.1 [1]; Brown & Srawley [6]

A high-strength steel needs a specimen a few millimetres thick, while a tough pressure-vessel steel at room temperature would need a block about 0.4 m thick and 0.8 m wide, weighing a few tonnes. That is why tough materials are tested with J or CTOD instead (section 6 and the J-integral page).

Material data in the figure. Typical room-temperature values, not design allowables. AISI 4340 tempered at 260 °C: KIC = 50.0 MPa√m, σYS = 1640 MPa, Callister & Rethwisch, Table 8.1 [12]. Al 7075-T651 and annealed Ti-6Al-4V, L-T orientation: KIC = 29 and 66 MPa√m, σYS = 505 and 925 MPa, Dowling, Table 8.1 [13], which compiles data from Barsom & Rolfe, the ASM materials handbooks and MIL-HDBK-5. Pressure-vessel plate SA533B-1: σYS = 481 MPa at 24 °C and nil-ductility temperature −29 °C, Ritchie, Server & Wullaert [14]. Valid KIC values do not exist for this steel at room temperature, for the reason shown in the figure, so K is estimated from the ASME Section XI reference curve KIC = 36.5 + 22.783 exp[0.036(T − RTNDT)] MPa√m with T − RTNDT ≈ 24 − (−29) = 53 °C, which gives 190 MPa√m [15]. The curve is a lower bound and is capped at 220 MPa√m, so the real block would be at least this large.

Orientation. Toughness depends on the direction of the crack relative to the rolling or forging direction. The code has two letters: the first is the direction normal to the crack plane, the second the direction of crack growth, with L the main working direction, T the transverse and S the short transverse (thickness) direction. A C(T) cut from a plate with its crack plane normal to L and growing along T is reported as C(T)(L-T).

Section 3 · A sharp crack

Why the crack is grown in fatigue

The whole of LEFM assumes an ideally sharp crack, whose tip radius is zero (section 1 of the stress intensity page: as ρ → 0 the stress concentration Kt becomes the K singularity). A machined notch, even one cut by wire erosion or a razor, has a root radius of tens of micrometres. The material at a blunt notch must be loaded further before it fractures, so a notched specimen overestimates the toughness. A fatigue crack grown from the notch has a tip radius set only by the tiny plastic zone of the last cycles, which is as close to an ideal crack as can be made. The standard therefore requires a fatigue pre-crack [1].

The notch. The crack starter can be a straight-through slot ending in a V (root radius 0.08 mm or less), a chevron (radius up to 0.25 mm), or a slot ending in a drilled hole with a small sharp stress raiser. The chevron is cut by two saw or cutter passes at an angle, one from each face, so that on the crack plane the notch root is a V whose point lies at mid-thickness (included angle 120° at most, centred on the mid-plane within 0.005W). The ligament is very narrow at the point, so the stress there is high and the fatigue crack starts at a low load, then widens as it grows. Pre-cracking must continue until the crack has broken out of the chevron on both faces. The notch must be square to the faces within 2°.

The pre-crack. The specimen, fully heat treated, is cycled in the same fixture as the test, usually with a sine wave at the highest practical frequency (no frequency effect is known up to 100 Hz in air). The fatigue crack must extend at least 2.5% of W, and at least 1.3 mm, beyond a straight notch on both faces, and the total crack length must end between 0.45W and 0.55W. Growth follows the Paris law, so the number of cycles depends strongly on K:

(4)da/dN = C ΔKm,   ΔK = Kmax − Kmin = (1 − R) Kmax m ≈ 3 for most metals. The standard allows R between −1 and +0.1; a more negative R grows the crack faster but needs fixtures that push as well as pull. Typical pre-cracking takes 10⁴ to 10⁶ cycles, most of them to initiate the crack at the notch root. Cycle counts in the figure use illustrative Paris constants. Source: Paris & Erdogan [8]; ASTM E647 [5]

Controlling Kmax. A high Kmax would leave a large plastic zone and residual compressive stresses at the tip, which blunt it and raise the measured toughness. The standard sets three limits:

(5)Kmax ≤ 0.8 KIC,est (start),   then over the last 2.5% of a:   Kmax ≤ 0.6 KQ  and  Kmax/E ≤ 0.00032 √m The terminal stage covers at least 0.0125W of crack growth: draw two pencil lines on each face, one at the minimum final length and one 2.5% short of it, and reduce the load when the crack trace reaches the first one (97.5% of the final length). If pre-cracking is done at a temperature T₁ and the test at T₂, the limit becomes Kmax ≤ 0.6 (σYS1/σYS2) KQ. The 0.6 KQ condition can only be checked after the test, so it is set from the estimated KIC. Source: ASTM E399 Annex A2 [1]

Play the figure. Under load control Kmax rises as the crack grows (f(α) increases), so the load is stepped down for the terminal stage. Under displacement control the specimen becomes more compliant as the crack grows and Kmax falls by itself. For the titanium alloy the stiffness limit Kmax/E governs; for the aluminium alloy, 0.6 KQ. Watch both faces of a real specimen: if the crack starts on one side only, stop and turn the specimen around in the fixture. The crack length can be followed without stopping from the specimen compliance v/P [9], using the compliance equations given in the annexes of the standard.

Section 4 · The test record

Finding the load at the start of crack growth

The pre-cracked specimen is loaded in a clevis (C(T)) or a three-point bend fixture with rolling supports (SE(B)), so that the loading matches the K calibration. A double-cantilever clip gauge seated on knife edges at the crack mouth measures the opening v. Load is applied so that K increases at 0.55 to 2.75 MPa√m/s, about 0.3 to 1.7 kN/s for a standard 25 mm thick specimen, and the record P–v is plotted with an initial slope between 0.7 and 1.5 on the chart. At least three tests per material condition are recommended.

In a perfectly elastic specimen with a stationary crack the record would be a straight line. It bends because of plasticity at the tip and because the crack starts to grow, both of which increase the compliance. E399 defines the start of crack growth as an apparent crack extension of about 2%, which for standard specimens corresponds to a 5% drop in the secant slope:

(6)secant OP5: slope (P/v)5 = 0.95 (P/v)0;   PQ = P5, or the highest load before P5 if it is larger;   valid only if Pmax/PQ ≤ 1.10 (P/v)0 is the slope of the initial linear part. Ignore the slight nonlinearity often seen at the very start, and remove it if needed by a preliminary load–unload below the final pre-cracking K. Type I: smooth curve, PQ = P5. Type II: a small pop-in (a sudden burst of crack growth arrested by the surrounding material) before P5, PQ = pop-in load. Type III: fracture before the secant is reached, PQ = Pmax. Source: ASTM E399 §9.1 [1]

The Pmax/PQ ≤ 1.10 check rejects records where the specimen carries much more load after PQ. Then the nonlinearity comes from extensive plasticity rather than from the start of crack growth, and KQ would have no relation to KIC. Section 5 · KQ and validity

Calculate KQ, then check everything

After the test the specimen is broken open, if it has not already fractured, and the crack front is measured on the fracture surface. The fatigue pre-crack is smooth and often darker or heat tinted, so its front is easy to see against the final fracture.

Crack length. a is measured to 0.5% at the centre of the front and midway between the centre and each face (B/4, B/2, 3B/4), and the average of these three is used in eqs. (1) to (2c). The front must be reasonably straight: any two of the three readings within 10% of the average, the two surface readings within 15% of the average and within 10% of each other, no part of the front closer to the notch than 2.5% W or 1.3 mm, and the crack plane parallel to the specimen within 10°. A strongly curved front means the stress state through the thickness was not uniform during pre-cracking, which is the K(z) effect of section 7 of the stress intensity page.

(7)KQ = K(PQ, a, B, W),   KIC = KQ if all checks pass The checks in the calculator: a/W in 0.45–0.55 (§7.3.2.1), pre-crack length (§7.3.2.2), crack-front shape (§8.2.2), Pmax/PQ ≤ 1.10 (§9.1.2), B and a ≥ 2.5(KQ/σYS)² (§9.1.3), terminal pre-crack Kmax ≤ 0.6 KQ and Kmax/E ≤ 0.00032 √m (Annex A2). Source: ASTM E399 §8–9, Annexes A2–A4 [1]

Enter your own measurements, or pick an example. The result is reported as KIC, or as KQ "invalid according to section(s) … of ASTM E399", together with the specimen code and orientation, the pre-cracking Kmax and the cycles of the terminal stage, the three crack lengths, the test temperature and humidity, the loading rate, the record, Pmax/PQ and the fracture appearance.

Fracture appearance. Note how much of the fracture surface is flat and how much is slanted shear lips near the faces. A fully flat fracture is plane strain; wide shear lips mean that the faces were close to plane stress, as in the through-thickness model of section 7 of the stress intensity page. E399 asks for the proportion of oblique fracture (B − f)/B to be reported.

Even an invalid test gives useful information. E399 asks for the specimen strength ratio Rsx, which compares the maximum load with the plastic-collapse load of the ligament: Rsc = 2Pmax(2W + a) / [B(W − a)² σYS] for C(T) and DC(T), Rsa = 2Pmax(3X + 2W + a) / [B(W − a)² σYS] for A(T), and Rsb = 6PmaxW / [B(W − a)² σYS] for SE(B). It ranks the toughness of materials tested with the same specimen size. For materials too tough for a valid KIC, J and the J-dominance conditions of the J-integral page, section 6 take over: the size requirement drops from 2.5(K/σYS)² = 2.5 E′J/σYS², several hundred times J/σYS, to 10 JQ/σY in ASTM E1820 [3]. The specimen K calibrations of eqs. (1) to (2c) can also be checked with finite elements (K and J in FE software).
