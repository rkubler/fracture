The J-integral

When the plastic zone is no longer small, K stops describing the crack tip. The J-integral replaces it. This page defines J term by term, integrates it live around contours you choose, shows why it does not depend on the path, and links every step back to K for elastic materials.

Section 1 · Why a new parameter

K needs small-scale yielding; ductile materials break it

LEFM works while the plastic zone rp = (1/π)(K/σys)² sits deep inside the region where the elastic K field holds. Tough, ductile materials such as structural steels, stainless steels and aluminium alloys at room temperature often yield over a zone comparable to the crack length or the ligament before they fail. Then the K field is destroyed over the very region that controls fracture, and K measured or computed elastically no longer characterises the crack tip.

RegimePlastic zoneParameter that controls the tip
Small-scale yieldingrp ≪ a, W − a, BK (and J = K²/E′)
Elastic–plasticrp comparable to the ligamentJ (or CTOD)
Fully plasticwhole ligament yieldsJ, with care: limit-load and constraint effects

Rice [1] proposed J in 1968, building on Eshelby's energy–momentum tensor [2]; Cherepanov [3] reached the same integral independently. J is defined for any nonlinear elastic material, it reduces exactly to the elastic energy release rate G = K²/E′ when the material is linear, and Hutchinson, Rice and Rosengren [4][5] showed that it is the amplitude of the crack-tip field in a hardening plastic material. That makes J the elastic–plastic counterpart of K.

Section 2 · Definition

J is an energy flux into the crack tip

(1)J = ∫Γ ( W dy − Ti ∂ui/∂x ds ) Γ is any path from the lower to the upper crack face around the tip, anticlockwise; x is along the crack. Source: Rice [1]; Anderson [11] §3.2

Section 3 · Computing J

Integrate around the tip and watch the terms

The figure evaluates equation (1) numerically on the exact mode I field of Williams and Irwin [12][13], the same field used on the stress intensity page, for a steel with E = 200 GPa and ν = 0.3. The tracer moves along Γ, showing the outward normal n (black arrow) and the traction T (magenta arrow). The lower chart accumulates the two terms as the tracer goes round.

  1. At each point of Γ, compute the stresses σij, the strains εij from Hooke's law, and W = ½σijεij.
  2. Compute the outward normal n, the traction Ti = σijnj, and ∂ui/∂x from the displacement field.
  3. Add W dy − Ti ∂ui/∂x ds over all 1 200 path segments.

Change the radius, switch to a rectangle and move its edges, or add a T-stress. Pick σxx or von Mises in the colour map to see what T does: it adds only to σxx, so the σyy and τxy maps do not move, while σxx shifts everywhere. The von Mises map uses a fixed scale with red at σY = 500 MPa, so the red region is an elastic estimate of the plastic zone: it grows when T < 0 and shrinks when T > 0, even though J stays the same. This is the constraint effect described below. The T-stress slider is the constant σxx = T term of the Williams expansion (second term, see the stress intensity page). It is not the traction vector Ti = σijnj of equation (1), which is the magenta arrow. The two terms change, sometimes a lot, but their sum stays equal to K²/E′. Choose the loop that does not enclose the tip and J falls to zero. That is path independence, explained in the next section.

What the T-stress means physically

T is a uniform stress parallel to the crack, the first non-singular term of the Williams expansion: σxx = K/√(2πr)·fxx(θ) + T [12]. It is the same at every distance r and comes from the remote loading and the geometry, not from the singularity. A centre crack under uniaxial tension normal to it has T = −σ; under biaxial loading T = σxx∞ − σyy∞. Deep edge cracks in bending (SENB, CT) have T > 0; short cracks and centre-cracked panels in tension have T < 0. Geometries are compared through the biaxiality ratio BT = T√(πa)/K [21].

Why J does not change. A stress parallel to the crack does not pull the faces apart, so it feeds no energy to the tip. In equation (1) the T² part is a uniform field with no singularity inside Γ, so it integrates to zero, and the K·T cross term cancels between the upper and lower halves of Γ by symmetry. That is what the slider shows: the two terms move, the sum stays K²/E′.

Why it still matters: constraint. T changes the triaxiality ahead of the tip [17]. With T > 0 (high constraint) the hydrostatic stress is higher, the plastic zone is smaller and leans forward, and the material shows its lower-bound toughness, as in deep-cracked test specimens. With T < 0 (loss of constraint) the hydrostatic stress drops, the plastic zone grows, and the apparent toughness rises above KIC or JIc, as in shallow cracks and thin panels. Two cracks with the same K or J can therefore fail at different loads. This is the basis of two-parameter fracture mechanics: J–T [18] and J–Q [19].

Crack path. T also affects directional stability: with T > 0 a straight crack tends to kink away from its plane, with T < 0 it stays straight [20].

In a finite element code J is evaluated the same way, usually converted to an equivalent area integral over a ring of elements (the domain integral method), which is more accurate than a line integral through element nodes. The FE software page runs such a calculation live on a spider-web mesh.

Section 4 · Why J does not depend on Γ

Path independence in four steps

  1. Closed contour. Take two paths Γ₁ (outer) and Γ₂ (inner) and join them along the crack faces into the closed loop Γ* shown in the figure. The tip, where the field is singular, stays outside Γ*.
  2. Divergence theorem. With dy = nx ds the integrand of (1) is a flux, so
    (3)∮Γ* ( W nx − σij nj ∂ui/∂x ) ds = ∫A [ ∂W/∂x − ∂(σij ∂ui/∂x)/∂xj ] dA Source: Rice [1]; Anderson [11] Appendix 3.2
  3. The area integrand vanishes. If W depends only on strain (elastic, or deformation plasticity), ∂W/∂x = σij ∂εij/∂x. Compatibility gives σij∂εij/∂x = σij ∂/∂xj(∂ui/∂x), and equilibrium without body forces, ∂σij/∂xj = 0, makes this equal to ∂(σij∂ui/∂x)/∂xj. The bracket is zero, so ∮Γ* = 0.
  4. The crack faces contribute nothing. On flat, traction-free faces dy = 0 and Ti = 0, so both terms vanish on Γ⁺ and Γ⁻. What is left is J(Γ₁) − J(Γ₂) = 0.
(4)J(Γ₁) = J(Γ₂) for any two paths around the tip The "Loop away from tip" contour in Section 3 is a closed Γ* with nothing singular inside: its J is zero up to round-off. Source: Rice [1]

Path independence is the practical strength of J. Γ can be taken far from the tip, where the stresses and strains are accurately known from a coarse mesh or from measurements, and J still describes the tip.

When it fails: unloading or non-proportional loading in real (incremental) plasticity, because W is then no longer a function of strain alone; crack growth, which unloads material behind the tip; thermal strains; body forces; tractions or pressure on the crack faces; and curved crack faces. Extra terms must then be added, or J is evaluated close to the tip.

Section 5 · J for elastic materials

In a linear elastic body, J is K²/E′

Because J = −dΠ/da = G, Irwin's relation between G and K [13] gives J directly from the stress intensity factors. Section 3 verifies it numerically: whatever the contour, the integral of the Williams field returns K²/E′.

(5)J = G = KI² / E′   (mode I) E′ = E in plane stress, E/(1 − ν²) in plane strain. Units: (MPa√m)²/MPa = MPa·m = MJ/m². Source: Rice [1]; Irwin [13]
(6)J = (KI² + KII²)/E′ + KIII²/2μ Mixed mode: the energies add, the K's do not. Source: Anderson [11] §3.2
(7)KJ = √(J E′) The reverse relation. A toughness measured as JIc on an elastic–plastic test is often reported as an equivalent KJc so it can be compared with KIC. Source: ASTM E1820 [9]

Default values: the Ti-6Al-4V example from section 6 of the stress intensity page, K = 38.6 MPa√m, E = 114 GPa, ν = 0.34. The same crack, the same energy: J is just K written as an energy.

Section 6 · Elastic–plastic crack tips

J is the amplitude of the HRR field

Model the material with the Ramberg–Osgood law [7], where n is the strain-hardening exponent (n = 1 is linear, n → ∞ is perfectly plastic):

(8)ε/ε0 = σ/σ0 + α (σ/σ0)n σ0 is a reference (yield) stress, ε0 = σ0/E, α a dimensionless constant. Source: Ramberg & Osgood [7]

For J to be finite and path-independent, W ∝ σε must vary as 1/r near the tip. Combined with (8), this fixes the singularity found by Hutchinson [4] and Rice and Rosengren [5]:

(9)σij = σ0 ( EJ / (α σ0² In r) )1/(n+1) σ̃ij(n, θ) εij = α ε0 ( EJ / (α σ0² In r) )n/(n+1) ε̃ij(n, θ). In (about 4 to 6 for practical n) and the angular functions σ̃ij, ε̃ij are tabulated numerically. Source: Hutchinson [4]; Rice & Rosengren [5]; Shih [6]

J plays exactly the role K plays in LEFM: it is the single amplitude of a universal near-tip field. Set n = 1 in the figure and the stress slope returns to −½, the elastic 1/√r singularity, with J = K²/E′. The product σε always has slope −1, whatever n.

What is rref? It is the reference radius used to normalise the figure: each line is scaled to 1 at r = rref, so only the slopes matter. A natural choice from eq. (9) is rref = EJ / (α σ0² In) = J / (α σ0 ε0 In), the radius where the amplitude factor in brackets equals 1. It grows in proportion to J, so a higher load enlarges the HRR field without changing its slopes.

(10)J = dn σ0 δt  ⇔  δt = J / (m σ0) Crack-tip opening displacement (CTOD). dn ranges from about 0.3 to 0.8 depending on n and the stress state, so m ≈ 1 to 2. Source: Shih [6]

K-dominance and J-dominance

A single parameter describes the crack tip only where its field actually holds. Around the tip, from the inside out, there are five regions. The second figure in this section shows them along the line ahead of the tip:

K-dominance needs an elastic annulus outside the plastic zone where σij ≈ K/√(2πr) fij(θ). This is small-scale yielding, and then J = K²/E′. ASTM E399 [10] enforces it with a, B, b ≥ 2.5 (K/σY)², about fifty times the plastic zone size. The annulus has an outer limit too: beyond about 0.1b the other terms of the elastic solution take over and the field depends on the geometry. In the figure the solid curve leaves the K line there, flattening towards the remote stress σ∞ in centre-cracked tension and falling to zero near mid-ligament in bending, where the stress becomes compressive. J-dominance is weaker: it only needs the HRR field of eq. (9) inside the plastic zone, between the blunting zone and the region influenced by the free boundaries. The K annulus can be gone and J still governs the tip.

(11)b, B ≥ M · J / σ0,   M ≈ 25 (bend), M ≈ 200 (tension) b is the uncracked ligament. Deep-cracked bend and CT specimens keep high constraint, centre-cracked panels in tension lose it early. ASTM E1820 [9] uses M = 10 for JIc. The blunting zone, about 2δt ≈ 1–2 J/σ0, sets the inner limit [24]. Source: McMeeking & Parks [22]; Shih & German [23]

When the ligament is too small, the fields of different geometries at the same J no longer agree. A second parameter measures the difference, either the elastic T-stress [18] or the triaxiality parameter Q [19], see the T-stress box in section 3:

(12)σij ≈ (σij)HRR + Q σ0 δij   (|θ| ≤ π/2, J/σ0 < r < 5J/σ0) Strictly, the reference field is the small-scale yielding solution with T = 0 (a modified boundary layer computed by FE); the HRR field is its leading term and is used here for simplicity. Q is read at θ = 0, r = 2J/σ0: Q = (σθθ − (σθθ)ref)/σ0. Q ≈ 0 for a high-constraint geometry (J-dominance holds). Q < 0 means lower stress triaxiality and a higher apparent toughness: Jc becomes a function Jc(Q), the J–Q toughness locus. In the figure the solid curve drops below the HRR line by Qσ0 as the load rises; the orange bar at r = 2J/σ0 marks that gap and gives Q. The values are schematic, chosen to show the trend for each geometry, not computed. Source: O'Dowd & Shih [19]; Betegón & Hancock [18]

Section 7 · Measuring J

J from the area under a load–displacement record

Path independence means J can also be obtained from global quantities. For a deeply cracked bend specimen almost all plastic work goes into the ligament, and Rice, Paris and Merkle [8] showed that J follows from the area under the load–displacement curve. ASTM E1820 [9] splits J into an elastic part, computed from K exactly as in LEFM, and a plastic part:

(13)J = K²(1 − ν²)/E + ηpl Upl / (B b0) Upl is the plastic area under P–Δ (shaded), B the thickness, b0 = W − a0 the ligament, ηpl = 1.9 for a single-edge bend specimen. Source: Rice, Paris & Merkle [8]; ASTM E1820 [9]
(14)K = P S / (B W3/2) · f(a/W) f(a/W) = 3(a/W)½[1.99 − (a/W)(1 − a/W)(2.15 − 3.93a/W + 2.7(a/W)²)] / [2(1 + 2a/W)(1 − a/W)3/2], three-point bending with span S = 4W. Source: ASTM E399 [10]; Tada et al. [16]

The record in the figure is illustrative: a steel bend bar with W = 50 mm, B = 25 mm, a0/W = 0.5, S = 200 mm, E = 200 GPa, σY = 500 MPa, with a Ramberg–Osgood-type plastic response. Early in the test J ≈ Jel = K²/E′, the LEFM value. As the ligament yields, Jpl takes over and K computed elastically underestimates the crack driving force.

In design, J is estimated without a test from handbook solutions of the same Jel + Jpl form (the EPRI method of Kumar, German and Shih [14]). The fracture criterion is J ≥ JIc, or a J–R resistance curve for stable tearing, as introduced by Begley and Landes [15].
